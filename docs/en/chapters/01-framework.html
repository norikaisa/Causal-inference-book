1  Framework for Causal Inference

1.1 Individual Causal Effects

Zhang San is a patient with intracerebral hemorrhage. On October 1, he underwent surgery for an acute hemorrhage and was alive one month later. Imagine that he had not received surgery on October 1: he might have died within the following month. With this information, most people would agree that surgery saved Zhang San’s life. Surgery therefore had a causal effect on his one-month survival.

Another patient, Li Si, also experienced intracerebral hemorrhage on October 1. He did not undergo surgery and died one month later. We may imagine that, had Li Si received surgery on October 1, he might have survived. Surgery therefore could also have had a causal effect on Li Si’s one-month survival.

These examples illustrate causal reasoning: we compare the outcome when an action is taken with the outcome when it is not taken, even though the latter comparison is usually only imagined. If the two outcomes differ, the action has a causal effect on the outcome; otherwise, it does not. In epidemiology, statistics, economics, and other social sciences, such an action is often called an intervention, exposure, or treatment.

To express this intuition mathematically, consider a binary treatment variable—common in clinical research—where \(T=1\) denotes treatment and \(T=0\) denotes no treatment or control, and a binary outcome where \(Y=1\) denotes death and \(Y=0\) denotes survival. The outcome under treatment is written as:

Equation 1.1 Potential outcome under treatment

\[Y^{T=1}\]

The outcome without treatment is written as:

Equation 1.2 Potential outcome without treatment

\[Y^{T=0}\]

For Zhang San or Li Si, the causal effect is:

Equation 1.3 Individual causal effect

\[Y^{T=1}-Y^{T=0}\]

If this difference is zero, treatment has no causal effect for that person; if it is nonzero, treatment has a causal effect. Because this effect applies to Zhang San or Li Si specifically, it is called an individual causal effect.

The two examples require us to imagine the treatment opposite to the one actually received: what would have happened if Zhang San had not undergone surgery, or if Li Si had undergone surgery? The alternative to what actually happened is called a potential outcome; it is also called a counterfactual outcome to emphasize that it represents a situation that did not occur. In almost all settings, however, only one potential outcome can be observed for each person. We know that Zhang San had surgery and survived, and that Li Si did not have surgery and died; the other potential outcome is unobserved. In this sense, an individual causal effect is not identifiable. This framework is known as the potential-outcomes or counterfactual framework. It can be traced to Neyman’s 1923 analysis of randomized experiments and Rubin’s 1974 extension to observational studies, and is therefore also called the Neyman–Rubin potential-outcomes model. Holland (1986) described the fact that each individual can contribute at most one observed potential outcome as the fundamental problem of causal inference.

1.2 Causation and Association

A well-known statistical maxim is that “association does not imply causation.” Ice-cream sales are associated with attendance at swimming pools. It is easy to see why they move together, but we would not say that higher ice-cream sales cause more people to visit swimming pools, or vice versa. In ordinary circumstances, hot weather increases ice-cream sales and also encourages people to go swimming. Both variables share a common cause: warm weather. This idea is discussed in detail in Chapter 8.

Case 1.1

Suppose that the emergency neurosurgery department at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, admitted 10 patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (Table 1.1). Five underwent surgery and five received non-surgical treatment. Mortality was lower among surgical patients (1/5, 20%) than among patients managed conservatively (3/5, 60%). We can therefore say that surgery is associated with mortality, and it is easy to make the erroneous inference that surgery reduces mortality in intracerebral hemorrhage. Yet we do not know whether the surgical patients would also have survived without surgery, or whether surgery could have saved the lives of those who did not receive it. If conservatively managed patients were too severely ill to tolerate surgery, the association is unsurprising.

4 Surgery Survived Survived ?

In the counterfactual questions introduced in the preceding section, we ask, “What would happen if an individual received treatment?” and “What would happen if the individual did not receive treatment?” Association, by contrast, describes observations in the real world: “What outcome was observed among treated individuals?” and “What outcome was observed among untreated individuals?” Under what conditions can real-world observations support causal inference? Chapter 2 provides one answer: randomized controlled trials.

If these treatments were to be introduced in China, which would be preferable? Overall, treatment A appears preferable because its response rate is 70%, compared with 60% for treatment B. Yet within both mild and severe disease, treatment B has the higher response rate: 90% versus 80% in mild disease, and 50% versus 20% in severe disease.

The apparent paradox arises because looking only at the “overall” column favors A, whereas considering both severity strata favors B. Which treatment should be chosen?

Usually, treatment B should be chosen. Both common sense and the data indicate that response rates are higher in mild than in severe disease. Most mildly ill patients received A (1,000 received A and 100 received B), whereas most severely ill patients received B (300 received B and 200 received A). In other words, A’s higher overall response rate reflects the fact that A was used mainly in mildly ill patients. Disease severity confounds the relationship between treatment and response. To correct this confounding, treatment and outcome must be compared within severity strata. This leads to choosing treatment B.

There is, however, a less common possibility. Treatment B might be so scarce that patients must wait a long time to receive it, while A is immediately available. Because COVID-19 pneumonia can progress over time, waiting for B could allow mildly ill patients to become severely ill and reduce B’s overall response rate. Thus, even if B is more effective within each severity stratum, choosing B might worsen the overall outcome if access to it causes harmful delay.

This example shows that surface associations alone do not guide treatment decisions. Understanding the underlying causal relationships is necessary to resolve Simpson’s paradox and to guide clinical and public-health decisions.

1.5 Conditions for Estimating an Average Causal Effect

The preceding section defined the average causal effect in a hypothetical setting in which every patient receives surgery and in another setting in which every patient receives no surgery. In reality, we cannot observe both potential outcomes for the same patient; we only have the first three columns of Table 1.3. As discussed above, directly comparing mortality in surgical and conservatively managed patients yields an association. Under what conditions can that association be interpreted as an average causal effect?

Four conditions are required.

  1. Exchangeability. We cannot directly compare surgical and conservatively managed patients because the groups may not be comparable. Imagine the surgical patients as an original file and the non-surgical patients as copy-and-paste duplicates of that file: intuitively, the two groups would then be comparable because they would be identical in all respects other than treatment. If either the original or its duplicate received surgery, its outcome would be the same; the same would hold if either received conservative treatment. This is exchangeability. Complete exchangeability is generally unrealistic in observed data, because the two groups are rarely identical in every characteristic other than treatment. Randomized controlled trials can make the assumption more plausible, as discussed in the next chapter.

  2. Conditional exchangeability. Complete exchangeability is unrealistic in observational data, but exchangeability may hold after conditioning on relevant variables. In the example in Section 3, one may control for disease severity by comparing the response rates of treatments A and B only among patients with mild COVID-19 pneumonia. Although response rates may be related to severity overall, within the mild stratum they are no longer compared across different severity levels. Controlling for severity can therefore produce conditional exchangeability between treatment groups.

    Conditional exchangeability, also called no unmeasured confounding, is a central causal-inference assumption. It is more realistic than complete exchangeability, but it cannot usually be verified: unmeasured confounders may be absent from the data. Intuitively, the best practical approach is to observe and record as many relevant variables as possible.

  3. Positivity (overlap). When comparing surgery with conservative treatment for intracerebral hemorrhage, there must be patients who receive surgery and patients who receive conservative treatment. If everyone receives surgery, the average causal effect cannot be estimated. Positivity is also required within relevant subgroups. For example, patients with hematoma volumes below 10 mL generally do not undergo surgery; in that group, the average causal effect of surgery versus no surgery cannot be learned from the data (and may not be clinically necessary). In other words, the covariate distributions of the treatment and control groups must overlap, so that every subgroup relevant to the comparison has some probability of receiving each treatment.

  4. Consistency. Return to surgery for intracerebral hemorrhage. Defining treatment merely as “surgery” or “no surgery” may be too vague to satisfy consistency. Surgery may include craniotomy with hematoma evacuation, burr-hole drainage, or intracranial-pressure monitoring; it may also depend on the surgeon and on whether conventional microscopic or newer endoscopic techniques are used. Causal inference therefore requires a precise definition of treatment, including its mode, method, dose, and timing. This requirement is closely related to Rubin’s stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA): an individual’s potential outcome is not affected by treatments received by others (no interference), and each treatment corresponds to one well-defined version. Exchangeability, conditional exchangeability, positivity, and consistency are identification conditions that allow associations to be interpreted as causal effects. Hernán and Robins (2020) discuss them systematically; the epidemiologic formulation of exchangeability can be traced to Greenland and Robins (1986), and Cole and Frangakis (2009) discuss the precise meaning of consistency.

Case 1.3

One study question concerns postmenopausal hormone therapy and cardiovascular events. Consider two studies: an observational study and a randomized controlled trial. In the randomized trial, 16,000 menopausal women were randomly assigned to initiate hormone therapy or not to use hormones. The published results suggested that initiating hormones increased the risk of cardiovascular events by 20%, with a hazard ratio of 1.24.

In an observational study, women currently using hormones had a 30% lower risk of cardiovascular events than women who had never used hormones, with a hazard ratio of 0.68.

The results appear contradictory, and randomized trials are generally considered more reliable. Why did the studies differ? The interventions of interest were different. The observational study compared women currently using hormones with women who had never used them; the randomized trial compared initiating hormones with not initiating hormones. Thus, the causal question in the observational study was not the causal question in the randomized trial.

Reanalysis of the randomized-trial results shows that, during the first two years after hormone initiation, cardiovascular-event risk was indeed increased. With more than five years of follow-up, however, cardiovascular-event risk was lower among these women. Participants who experienced cardiovascular events early had already reached the outcome and were no longer under follow-up; women remaining at later times were inherently less likely to experience such events. The observational study had a similar problem, because hormone users who experienced cardiovascular events could not enter the comparison as current users, producing an apparently protective association.

When the observational study was reanalyzed by restricting the comparison to initiators and non-initiators, its conclusion agreed with that of the randomized trial: women initiating hormones had increased cardiovascular risk. This illustrates why defining the causal question is essential.

The two studies in Case 1.3 are the classic analysis of Hernán et al. (2008). When investigators designed and analyzed the observational Nurses’ Health Study to emulate the randomized Women’s Health Initiative (WHI), and allowed risk to vary with time since hormone initiation, the results became more consistent. This illustrates target-trial emulation: first specify the hypothetical randomized trial to be emulated, then use observational data to emulate it, thereby avoiding design biases such as inappropriate inclusion of prevalent users (Hernán and Robins, 2016).

This requirement is closely related to Rubin's stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA): an individual's potential outcome is not affected by treatments received by others (no interference), and each treatment corresponds to one well-defined version. Exchangeability, conditional exchangeability, positivity, and consistency are identification conditions that allow associations to be interpreted as causal effects. Hernán and Robins (2020) discuss them systematically; the epidemiologic formulation of exchangeability can be traced to Greenland and Robins (1986), and Cole and Frangakis (2009) discuss the precise meaning of consistency.

**Case 1.3**

One study question concerns postmenopausal hormone therapy and cardiovascular events. Consider two studies: an observational study and a randomized controlled trial. In the randomized trial, 16,000 menopausal women were randomly assigned to initiate hormone therapy or not to use hormones. The published results suggested that initiating hormones increased the risk of cardiovascular events by 20%, with a hazard ratio of 1.24.

In an observational study, women currently using hormones had a 30% lower risk of cardiovascular events than women who had never used hormones, with a hazard ratio of 0.68.

The results appear contradictory, and randomized trials are generally considered more reliable. Why did the studies differ? The interventions of interest were different. The observational study compared women currently using hormones with women who had never used them; the randomized trial compared initiating hormones with not initiating hormones. Thus, the causal question in the observational study was not the causal question in the randomized trial.

Reanalysis of the randomized-trial results shows that, during the first two years after hormone initiation, cardiovascular-event risk was indeed increased. With more than five years of follow-up, however, cardiovascular-event risk was lower among these women. Participants who experienced cardiovascular events early had already reached the outcome and were no longer under follow-up; women remaining at later times were inherently less likely to experience such events. The observational study had a similar problem, because hormone users who experienced cardiovascular events could not enter the comparison as current users, producing an apparently protective association.

When the observational study was reanalyzed by restricting the comparison to initiators and non-initiators, its conclusion agreed with that of the randomized trial: women initiating hormones had increased cardiovascular risk. This illustrates why defining the causal question is essential.

The two studies in Case 1.3 are the classic analysis of Hernán et al. (2008). When investigators designed and analyzed the observational Nurses' Health Study to emulate the randomized Women's Health Initiative (WHI), and allowed risk to vary with time since hormone initiation, the results became more consistent. This illustrates target-trial emulation: first specify the hypothetical randomized trial to be emulated, then use observational data to emulate it, thereby avoiding design biases such as inappropriate inclusion of prevalent users (Hernán and Robins, 2016).
