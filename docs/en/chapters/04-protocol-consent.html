4  Writing Randomized Controlled Trial Protocols and Informed Consent

Writing a protocol and participant informed-consent materials is an important step before conducting an RCT. This chapter sets out the key elements and provides a model protocol. Protocols should follow the SPIRIT 2013 statement (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials), which provides a 33-item checklist. A trial should also be prospectively registered on a public registry, such as ClinicalTrials.gov or the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, before participant enrollment (Chan et al. 2013).

4.1 Writing an RCT protocol

4.1.1 1. Research background

The protocol should provide sufficient background to demonstrate the importance and necessity of the question, expected benefits, and relevant references and data. It should describe prior laboratory and animal research, scientific literature, and other information supporting the rationale; it should summarize domestic and international progress and identify the innovation of the proposed study.

A useful sequence is: define the research question; describe the current situation and its deficiencies; review relevant domestic and international progress; explain what the study will do, its innovations, and its clinical or societal value; describe prior work and the conditions supporting the scientific rationale, feasibility, and regulatory compliance; and assess possible risks, the justification for choosing the population, potential benefit, and whether benefit outweighs risk.

4.1.2 2. Objectives

Every study needs clear objectives: the question it will answer or problem it will solve. In drug research, objectives generally concern safety and efficacy. Too many objectives make data collection and analysis more complex and can compromise feasibility.

4.1.3 3. Design, randomization, and blinding

The protocol should state how the allocation sequence will be generated, for example by computer-generated random numbers; how the sequence will be implemented, for example through a central telephone system; and how it will be kept unknown before allocation. It should confirm clinical equipoise—the genuine uncertainty about comparative treatment effects that makes random allocation ethically acceptable.

Blinding can reduce measurement bias. State who will be blinded (participants, investigators, outcome assessors), whether the study is single- or double-blind, and how blinding will be implemented. For an unblinded study, explain why and describe bias-control measures. Specify unblinding procedures and emergency code-breaking arrangements.

4.1.4 4. Inclusion, exclusion, withdrawal, and stopping criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria usually address demographic information (age, sex, race), health and disease requirements, concomitant medication, previous treatment, and other relevant factors. Disease and severity requirements should use recognized diagnostic standards rather than standards altered to suit the study, because the latter harms reliability. In addition to the target indication, consider whether study procedures or interventions could harm patients of a given severity and whether risks are controllable. Restrictions on comorbidities, behavior, and lifestyle (such as smoking or alcohol) can control confounding, clarify treatment effects, and reduce unexpected serious adverse events; too many restrictions make the sample unrepresentative and limit generalizability.

For safety, participants normally may not join more than one clinical study at a time. Specify a washout period after prior drug trials. People of reproductive potential generally need effective contraception during the study. If mutagenic or reproductive toxicity in men cannot be excluded, male participants should also use effective contraception.

Specify when a participant must stop treatment or leave the study, rather than when the whole project stops: unwillingness or inability to continue; discovery after enrollment that eligibility is not met; intolerable adverse events or serious adverse events for which continued participation has more risk than benefit; no improvement or deterioration after continuous treatment (with a defined time point for judging ineffectiveness); loss to follow-up; or use of protocol-prohibited drugs or treatments. Specify criteria for stopping the entire study: major protocol errors or serious deviations that prevent assessment; treatment-related adverse events making continuation inappropriate; a requirement from regulators, government, or the ethics committee; or other situations in which investigators consider continuation unsuitable or impossible.

4.1.5 5. Sample size

Calculate the number of participants needed to achieve the objective using statistical principles. Consider the minimum clinically important difference, variability of the primary measure, type I error (alpha; false positive), type II error (beta; false negative), one- or two-sided testing, the statistical test, and expected loss to follow-up.

4.1.6 6. Study procedures

RCTs usually include screening, intervention, and follow-up periods. Describe all study content and steps in detail, preferably with a flowchart. State clinical and laboratory assessments, their frequency, volume and number of blood or tissue specimens, and total specimen volume. State the route, method, frequency, and duration of intervention. For drugs, specify formulation, dose, route, administration method, frequency, course, and rules for concomitant medication; also describe accountability, use records, delivery, dispensing, storage, packaging, and labeling. Specify follow-up visits and content, and measures to support adherence, such as diary cards, adherence education, pill counts, and return of drug containers.

For drug interventions, specify rules for stopping, reducing, and resuming dose after adverse drug reactions, how reactions will be managed, how loss to follow-up is defined, and the process that follows it.

4.1.7 7. Efficacy assessment

Studies assessing efficacy require outcome measures. There may be primary and secondary outcomes, each with standards for measurement method, observation time, recording, and analysis.

4.1.8 8. Adverse events

Record in detail every clinical adverse event and abnormal laboratory result, evaluate its relationship to the study drug, and judge severity. An adverse event is any unfavorable medical occurrence or clinically meaningful laboratory abnormality during a trial, whether or not related to study treatment. For each, describe onset, duration, management, outcome, severity, and relationship to study drug.

A serious adverse event (SAE) is any unfavorable event at any dose that results in death, is life-threatening, requires or prolongs hospitalization, causes persistent or substantial disability/incapacity, causes a congenital anomaly/birth defect, or is an important medical event that may jeopardize the participant or require intervention to prevent one of these outcomes. On an SAE, investigators should immediately protect the participant and report it to the ethics committee and relevant authorities within 24 hours.

Classify relationship according to temporal sequence, reaction type, and response to withdrawal/rechallenge as definitely related, probably related, possibly related, possibly unrelated, or definitely unrelated. “Definitely related” requires evidence of exposure, a reasonable time relationship, a more plausible drug explanation than alternatives, a positive dechallenge, and, where feasible, a positive rechallenge. “Probably related” has exposure, a reasonable time relationship, a more plausible drug explanation, and positive dechallenge. “Possibly related” has exposure and a reasonable temporal relationship but may have another explanation and positive dechallenge. “Possibly unrelated” is better explained by another cause, with negative or unclear dechallenge. “Definitely unrelated” involves no exposure, no temporal relationship, or another definite cause.

4.1.9 9. Other items

Address construction of electronic case-report forms or research records; data entry and amendment; confidentiality of participant information and research data; quality control and assurance; ethics; recruitment and the consent process; anticipated progress and completion date; responsibilities of all parties; and other relevant rules.

4.2 Writing participant informed consent

  1. State that the participant is being invited to clinical research and that participation is voluntary.
  2. Describe national and international progress concerning the intervention.
  3. Introduce the design, including blinding, control, or crossover, and the planned number of participants.
  4. Describe the main content, procedures and duration, number of follow-ups, tests or procedures and their frequency, possible allocation to different groups, and medication methods.
  5. Explain known and unknown adverse reactions and possible risks and discomfort from study procedures.
  6. Explain potential benefits, such as curing disease or preventing/slowing progression, or clearly state that there may be no direct benefit; describe alternative therapies outside the study.
  7. Explain whether intervention and examination costs are waived, whether travel reimbursement is available, and whether free treatment and compensation are available for study-related injury.
  8. Explain participant responsibilities and rights, including the right to decline or withdraw at any time after notifying the investigator, and confidentiality of personal information. The entire consent process must comply with the Declaration of Helsinki, ICH Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP), and China’s GCP ethics requirements. Consent must contain all these elements, be written in language the participant can understand, and be signed voluntarily after adequate information and without coercion (World Medical Association 2013).

## Writing participant informed consent

1. State that the participant is being invited to clinical research and that participation is voluntary.
2. Describe national and international progress concerning the intervention.
3. Introduce the design, including blinding, control, or crossover, and the planned number of participants.
4. Describe the main content, procedures and duration, number of follow-ups, tests or procedures and their frequency, possible allocation to different groups, and medication methods.
5. Explain known and unknown adverse reactions and possible risks and discomfort from study procedures.
